Early Decision vs. Early Action

What each one commits you to, and when applying early actually helps.

Nickolas CorreaFounder of Rumo Prep · November 2026 · 5 min read

On this page

  1. The options side by side
  2. Early Decision
  3. Early Action and Restrictive Early Action
  4. When applying early helps

Every fall, families face the same question: apply early, or wait? Applying early can mean an answer by mid-December, and sometimes better odds. But one of the early options is a promise. It pays to know exactly what your student is agreeing to before they click submit.

The short version
  • Early Decision is binding: if admitted with enough aid, your student attends and withdraws every other application
  • Early Action isn't binding: your student gets an earlier answer and has until May 1 to decide
  • Applying early helps most when your student has a clear first choice and an application that's ready by November

The options side by side

Here are the three early options at a glance. Each college sets its own dates and rules, so always check its website.

Early Decision

With Early Decision, your student applies to one first-choice school and promises to attend if admitted. They can apply to other colleges in the regular round, but according to BigFuture, they must withdraw those applications if the Early Decision school says yes. Students, parents and the school counselor typically sign an agreement. UVA's Common Data Set spells it out plainly: students admitted Early Decision must cancel their applications elsewhere and enroll.

The one widely recognized exception is money. The commitment assumes the college offers enough financial aid, so run that school's net price calculator before applying, and talk as a family about what you can afford. If comparing aid offers from several colleges matters to you, Early Decision probably isn't the right choice.

Some colleges also offer Early Decision II, a second binding round with a January deadline and a decision around February. It suits students who settle on a first choice later in the fall.

Your student can apply Early Decision to only one school at a time. An early application can end three ways: admitted, denied or deferred. According to BigFuture, a deferred student is reconsidered with the regular applicants and doesn't need to reapply, so a deferral isn't a no. It's a later answer. If your student is deferred, a short update on new grades or achievements, if the college accepts one, is usually the best next step.

Early Action and Restrictive Early Action

Early Action gives your student an earlier answer with no commitment. They can apply Early Action to as many schools as they like and still have until May 1 to decide. Several DMV favorites work this way. The University of Maryland's non-binding November 1 deadline is also the cutoff for automatic merit scholarship consideration, so applying early there can mean money, not just an earlier answer. UVA offers both Early Decision and Early Action, each due November 1.

Restrictive Early Action, also called single-choice early action, is still non-binding, but it limits where else your student can apply early. Harvard, for example, doesn't allow its early applicants to apply early to other private colleges, though they may still apply Early Action to public universities. Georgetown is different again: its Early Action applicants may apply to other Early Action programs, but not to any binding Early Decision program.

Early Action is also a smart way to apply to likely schools. An early yes from a school your student would be happy to attend can arrive before winter break, and it takes a lot of pressure off the rest of the year. To keep the rules straight for each school on the list:

  • Search each college's site for its "early action" or "early decision" policy.
  • Note what other early applications are allowed, especially Early Decision.
  • Write down the decision date and the reply deadline for each school.

When applying early helps

The numbers can look striking. For the Class of 2030, Brown admitted 890 of 5,406 Early Decision applicants, about 16.5%. In the regular round, it admitted 1,674 of 42,531, under 4%. Northwestern filled more than half of its class early.

Those gaps don't mean applying early guarantees a better chance. Early pools are different. They often include recruited athletes and students with especially strong records, and some colleges say they hold early applicants to the same standard. Georgetown, for instance, says it admits early applicants it would also admit in the regular round. Still, at colleges that offer Early Decision, applying that way is a real signal of commitment, and at schools like the University of Maryland, an early application brings scholarship consideration.

  • Apply early if your student has a clear first choice they've researched or visited, the application is strong by November, and the cost works for your family.
  • Wait for the regular round if the essays are rushed, if senior fall grades would make the application stronger, or if you need to compare financial aid offers.

If your student is leaning toward applying early, work backward from November 1. Teacher recommendations should be requested in the spring of junior year, the personal statement drafted by the end of August, any final test taken by early October, and the application reviewed by a trusted reader in late October. A student who starts in October is usually better off waiting for the regular round.

Apply early to a school you'd say yes to tomorrow, with an application you'd be proud to send today.
