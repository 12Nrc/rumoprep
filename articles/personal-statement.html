What makes a personal statement stand out

A weak opening, a strong one, and what admissions readers notice in the first few lines.

Nickolas CorreaFounder of Rumo Prep · November 2026 · 5 min read

On this page

  1. What readers notice first
  2. A weak opening and a strong one
  3. Finding your topic
  4. Editing without losing your voice

Admissions readers work through thousands of essays every season. Virginia Tech alone received a record 57,622 applications for fall 2025. Readers move quickly, so the first few lines of a personal statement shape how carefully the rest gets read. The good news is that a standout essay doesn't need a dramatic story. It needs a real one, told in your student's own voice.

The short version
  • Readers look for voice and specific detail, and they notice it in the first two or three sentences
  • A strong opening drops the reader into a moment; a weak one announces a theme
  • Edit for clarity, not polish: the final essay should still sound like a 17-year-old

What readers notice first

The Common App personal statement is one essay, 250 to 650 words, sent to every college on your student's list. The seven prompts haven't changed for 2026–27, and last year the most popular choice was "topic of your choice." In other words, the prompt matters far less than what your student does with it.

Readers aren't grading vocabulary. As one Yale admissions officer put it on the Yale admissions blog, a strong essay has "little to do with topic, and everything to do with reflection and voice." Georgia Tech's admissions blog makes a similar point: a student's voice should come through in the very first sentence or two. A reader should finish the opening knowing something specific about how this student sees the world.

In practice, readers respond to three things. Specific detail: real names, places and moments instead of general claims. Reflection: not just what happened, but what your student noticed, wondered or changed their mind about. Voice: sentences that sound like a particular person, including their humor, their hesitations and the way they actually talk.

A weak opening and a strong one

Here are two openings on the same topic, a student who volunteers with older neighbors. First, the kind readers see over and over:

Ever since I was young, I have always been passionate about helping others, and volunteering has taught me the importance of compassion and hard work.

Now a stronger version:

The third time Mrs. Alvarez asked me to fix her TV, I realized the TV wasn't broken. She just wanted someone to talk to on Tuesdays.

The first could have been written by thousands of applicants. It tells the reader what to think (passionate, compassionate, hard-working) without showing anything. The second starts in a specific moment, with a real person and a small surprise, and it makes the reader want the next sentence. Notice that it isn't dramatic. Ordinary moments, told honestly, often make the best essays.

A few openings show up so often that readers tend to skim past them: a dictionary definition ("Webster's defines leadership as..."), a famous quotation, or a sweeping statement about the world ("In today's society..."). If your student's draft starts that way, try cutting the first paragraph entirely. The real essay often starts in the second.

Finding your topic

The best topics are usually small and specific: one habit, one place, one relationship, one change of mind. A big topic ("my passion for science") tends to produce a big, vague essay. A small one ("the notebook where I log every bird I see on my walk to school") gives the reader something real. A few questions that help students find theirs:

  1. What do you do when no one is assigning it?
  2. What's something you used to believe and changed your mind about?
  3. What would your closest friend say you're unusually good at, or unusually obsessed with?
  4. Where do you go, online or in real life, when you have a free hour?

Some topics aren't off-limits but need extra care, simply because so many students write about them: the big game, a service trip, moving to a new school, or the loss of a grandparent. If your student chooses one, the details have to be unusually personal, and the essay should spend more time on what they think now than on what happened.

Skip the list of achievements. The activities section already covers those. The personal statement is the one place to show how your student thinks.

Editing without losing your voice

Good editing makes an essay clearer, not more impressive. Have your student read the draft out loud: any sentence they stumble over, or wouldn't say to a friend, needs rewriting. Replace general words with specific ones, and cut anything that repeats. Keep the circle of readers small, one or two trusted people at most. An Oberlin admissions counselor offers a useful rule: if someone's suggested edits don't sound like something your student would write, don't use them.

The same goes for AI. The Common App treats AI-written content as fraud, and some colleges, like Brown, don't allow AI in application content at all. Readers can usually tell when an essay has been smoothed into something generic. We cover the rules in What's changing in college admissions this year.

It helps to revise in rounds rather than all at once. The first revision is about content: is this the right story, and does the essay say something true about your student? The second is about structure: does it start in the right place and end with a thought rather than a summary? Only the last round should focus on sentences and word count. Most strong essays land between 500 and 650 words.

Finally, give the essay time. A draft written in July and revised in August is almost always better than one written the week before a November deadline. Since the prompts haven't changed, your student can start any time.
