What's changing in college admissions this year

Testing policies, essays in the age of AI, early application strategy: what's different now, and what it means for your student.

Nickolas CorreaFounder of Rumo Prep · November 2026 · 5 min read

On this page

  1. Testing policies
  2. Essays in the age of AI
  3. Early application strategy
  4. What this means for your student

If an older child or a friend's child applied to college a few years ago, a lot of what they learned is already out of date. The most selective colleges are bringing back the SAT and ACT, colleges have drawn firm lines on AI, and more seats are being filled in the fall. Here's what's different for students applying now, and what to do about it.

The short version
  • Testing: most colleges are still test-optional, but almost every Ivy and a growing list of selective schools now require the SAT or ACT
  • Essays: the Common App treats AI-written content as fraud, and some colleges now use AI to help read essays
  • Timing: selective colleges fill a large share of their classes in the fall rounds, so senior fall matters more than ever

Testing policies

Colleges use one of three testing policies. Test-required means your student must send an SAT or ACT score. Test-optional means your student decides; if they send scores, the college uses them. Test-blind (sometimes called test-free) means the college won't look at scores even if you send them. The University of California system is the best-known example.

Most colleges are still flexible. According to FairTest, about 93% of four-year colleges don't require scores from students applying this year. The most selective schools are moving the other way, though. Yale ended its "test-flexible" policy in May 2026 and again requires the SAT or ACT. Princeton and Columbia are test-optional for one more cycle, then will require scores from students applying in fall 2027. For today's juniors, that means every Ivy League school will expect a score.

Closer to home, the picture is different. UVA lets students applying for fall 2027 choose whether to share scores, the University of Maryland is test-optional through fall 2027, and Virginia Tech through fall 2028. Getting a score is also getting easier: under a new state law, Virginia public schools must now offer the SAT to juniors during the school day. The ACT has changed too. It's now shorter, and the science section is optional.

  • If a school requires scores: plan to test, ideally finishing by the end of junior year.
  • If a school is test-optional: send scores when they're in or above the middle range of admitted students. If they're lower, it's often better to apply without them.
  • If a school is test-blind: don't spend time on scores for that school.

To find a school's middle range, search for its Common Data Set. Section C lists the 25th and 75th percentile scores of enrolled students who sent them. Plenty of students still apply without scores: through November 1 this fall, the Common App saw 5% more applicants who reported no test score, and 2% fewer who did.

Essays in the age of AI

The Common App, which more than 1,000 colleges use, counts it as fraud when a student submits the substantive content or output of an artificial intelligence platform as their own work. Individual colleges add their own rules, and they don't all agree. Brown says AI isn't permitted under any circumstances in application content. Notre Dame allows AI for spelling, grammar and brainstorming, but says the writing itself must be the student's.

At the same time, colleges are starting to use AI on their side of the desk. Virginia Tech now pairs a human reader with an AI model to score its short-answer essays, and says admissions decisions are still made by people. With record application numbers, readers move fast. That makes it even more important that an essay sounds like a real person and not like a template.

An essay that sounds like everyone else's is easy to write and easy to forget. Your student's own details are the one thing no tool can supply.

The safest approach is simple: follow the strictest policy on your student's list. A spelling check is generally fine. Asking a tool to write, rewrite or "improve" sentences is not.

These rules cover more than the main essay. Notre Dame's policy, for example, applies to short answers, the activities list and anything else a student submits. Every word in the application should be your student's own.

Early application strategy

The early rounds, usually due November 1 or November 15, now decide a large share of seats at selective colleges. Northwestern admitted more than half of its Class of 2030 through Early Decision. At UVA, the binding Early Decision round has grown from 2,159 applicants in its first year to 5,108 this past year, and it filled about a quarter of the class, according to UVA Magazine. And through November 1 this fall, the Common App reported that applications to colleges admitting fewer than 25% of students were up 14%.

That doesn't mean every student should apply early. Early Decision is a binding commitment, and an early application only helps if it's strong. We explain the options in detail in Early Decision vs. Early Action. The practical takeaway for now: for many students, the real deadline is November 1 of senior year, not January.

Some colleges are moving their dates earlier too. Virginia Tech moved its early deadline from November 15 to November 1 for the 2025–26 cycle, partly so it could return decisions sooner. Check every early deadline on your student's list each year rather than assuming last year's still applies.

What this means for your student

None of this means the process has become impossible. Most colleges still admit most of their applicants, and the fundamentals haven't changed: strong grades in challenging classes, real involvement in a few activities, and essays that show who a student is. What has changed is the timing and the fine print.

The changes point in one direction: the important work happens earlier than it used to. A student who has a test score, a draft essay and a researched list by the end of the summer before senior year has real choices in the fall.

  1. Juniors, this spring: take the SAT or ACT (SAT dates are March 6, May 1 and June 5, 2027), so a score is ready even if some colleges on the list don't require it.
  2. Seniors, this winter: check each college's own website for its testing and AI policies before submitting regular decision applications.
  3. This week: ask your student which colleges on their list require scores and what each one says about AI. If neither of you knows, that's the first thing to look up.
