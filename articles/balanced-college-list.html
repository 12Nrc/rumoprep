How to build a balanced college list

Reach, target and likely schools: how to sort them and how many of each to apply to.

Nickolas CorreaFounder of Rumo Prep · November 2026 · 5 min read

On this page

  1. Reach, target and likely
  2. How many schools in total
  3. Where to find the numbers
  4. Fit beyond the numbers

A good college list means your student has real choices in April. The most common mistakes go in opposite directions. Some lists are all reaches with no backup. Others are so long that no application gets the attention it needs. A balanced list avoids both, and it's built on real numbers, not guesses.

The short version
  • Sort every school into reach, target or likely using published data
  • Aim for about 8 to 12 schools, with most of them targets
  • Check cost and fit, not just the odds of getting in

Reach, target and likely

Every school on the list should fit one of three categories, based on how your student's grades and scores compare with the students that college admits. One rule overrides everything else: a school that admits fewer than about one in five applicants is a reach for everyone, even a student with perfect grades.

CategoryWhat it meansHow many
ReachYour student is below the middle range of admitted students, or the school admits fewer than about 20% of applicants2 to 4
TargetYour student is within the middle range at a school that admits a good share of applicants3 to 5
LikelyYour student is above the middle range at a school that admits most applicants, and would be happy to go there2 to 3

One caution: at test-optional schools, the published score ranges only include students who chose to send scores, so they tend to run high.

Grades are harder to compare, because high schools and colleges calculate GPAs differently. The most reliable guide is your own school's history. If students with grades like your student's have usually been admitted to a college, it's probably a target. If they've mostly been turned down, treat it as a reach.

How many schools in total

There's no perfect number, but there's a sensible range. The College Board suggests three reaches, two matches and one safety, adding one or two more if needed, for six to eight in total. Last year, the average Common App applicant applied to about seven colleges, according to its end-of-season report. Students aiming at highly selective schools often apply to 8 to 12.

Going much past 12 usually backfires. Many selective colleges ask for their own supplemental essays, and every extra school spreads your student's time thinner. A shorter list of strong applications beats a long list of rushed ones.

Early applications change the math in a good way. If your student is admitted Early Decision in December, the rest of the list falls away. Even so, build the full list before November. Waiting until a December decision to start the other applications leaves only a few weeks over the holidays to write them.

Where to find the numbers

You don't need to rely on rankings or rumors. The real numbers are public and free:

  • The Common Data Set: most colleges publish one each year. Search for the college's name plus "Common Data Set." Section C lists applicants, admits, early decision numbers and the middle range of test scores.
  • College Navigator and the College Scorecard: federal sites with admission rates, costs, graduation rates and earnings after graduation.
  • Net price calculators: every college that takes federal student aid must post one on its website. Enter your family's information to estimate what you'd actually pay, which is often very different from the sticker price.
  • Your high school's data: tools like Naviance or Scoir show how past students from your student's school did at each college.

Here's how that looks in practice. Say a college's Common Data Set shows the middle range of SAT scores is 1350 to 1500, and it admits about 30% of applicants. A student with a 1480 and strong grades is a solid target there. A student with a 1300 should count it as a reach, and decide whether to apply without a score if the school is test-optional.

For DMV families, cost is also local. Virginians and Marylanders get in-state tuition at their public universities, which makes schools like UVA, William & Mary, Virginia Tech and the University of Maryland strong options. DC residents can use the DC Tuition Assistance Grant, which helps cover the difference between in-state and out-of-state tuition at public colleges nationwide, up to $15,000 a year starting in 2026–27.

Fit beyond the numbers

Odds are only half of a good list. The other half is whether your student would actually thrive there. A few questions to talk through together:

  • Size: do they want to know their professors by name, or have hundreds of clubs to choose from?
  • Location: city, suburb or rural? How far from home feels right?
  • Majors: does the school have strong programs in what they want to study? Some programs admit students separately, with their own odds.
  • Cost: after the net price calculator, is it realistic for your family?
  • Campus life: housing, activities, and what students actually do on weekends.
The best list is one where every school is a place your student would be glad to go.

Build the list in stages. In the spring of junior year, start broad with 15 to 20 schools that look interesting. Over the summer, cut it to 8 to 12 using the numbers and the fit questions above. And don't overlook honors colleges at public universities, like the University of Maryland's Honors College. They can offer a small-school experience at a public-university price.

That last point matters most for likely schools. A likely school your student wouldn't attend isn't a backup at all. Visit one or two in person, and if your student can't picture themselves there, keep looking until they can.
